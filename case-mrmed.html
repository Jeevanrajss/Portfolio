Context

The project that already failed twice.

Mr.Med is an online e-pharmacy web app — search medicines, upload prescriptions, order, deliver. Standard brief, high stakes. By the time it reached me, it had already been through two designers and two rejected design rounds. The client was frustrated. The timeline was bleeding. The PM was running out of chances.

Then it landed on my desk.

The Problem

Clean screens. Wrong read of the brief.

Two previous design rounds had failed client review. Not because the designers were bad — but because the brief had been interpreted too literally. The screens were clean, but they didn't feel like a pharmacy. They felt like any generic e-commerce site with medicine SKUs swapped in.

"E-pharmacy has its own emotional grammar. Users aren't browsing for fun — they're often anxious, sometimes sick, usually in a hurry. The design had to acknowledge that."
The Constraints

No margin for another miss.

  • Zero room for another rejection. Third time had to land — project credibility was on the line
  • Feature set was already locked by the client — I couldn't redesign the scope, only the experience
  • Trust signals were make-or-break — pharmacy means prescriptions, genuine medicines, licensed pharmacists. Design had to carry that weight
  • Limited timeline — this was a recovery sprint, not a fresh kickoff
My Approach

Read the failures first. Then design.

Approach 01
Figure out why the previous rounds failed
Before touching a single screen, I reviewed both rejected designs and read every piece of client feedback. Pattern emerged fast: the client wanted something that felt trustworthy, medical, and urgent — not just visually clean.
Approach 02
Competitor study for emotion, not just patterns
Looked at 1mg, PharmEasy, Netmeds, Apollo Pharmacy — but studied how they made users feel safe. Color psychology, badge placement, prescription workflow clarity, pharmacist presence in the UI.
Approach 03
Design around the user's anxiety, not just their task
Every flow got reframed: "what is the user worried about at this step, and how does the UI reassure them?" That single question changed every design decision.
The Process

Trust at every step.

Homepage: clarity over cleverness. Prominent search, clear category browsing, visible trust markers — licensed pharmacy, genuine medicines, prescription upload. No hero carousels. No marketing fluff. People here have a job to do.

Prescription upload: the trust gate. Clear upload states, visible verification status, pharmacist review indicators. Users handing over a prescription need to know exactly what's happening at every step.

Product pages: information without overwhelm. Medicine details — composition, dosage, prescription required, alternatives — structured so both medical buyers and casual users find what they need without friction.

Cart and checkout: reassurance at every step. Delivery time estimates, prescription attached confirmations, genuine medicine badges. Every screen answered "am I getting the right thing, safely?"

Responsive-first. Designed for mobile web as primary — how people actually use pharmacy sites — desktop as secondary.

Learnings

What rescue projects teach you.

Learning 01
"Rejected designs usually aren't about aesthetics — they're about interpretation."
Two talented designers had already made it look good. The gap wasn't craft, it was context. Reading the rejection history before touching a screen is the single most important thing you can do on a rescue project.
Learning 02
"Design for the user's emotional state, not just their task."
People ordering medicine aren't in a neutral mood. Designing for anxious users in a hurry is very different from designing for casual browsers. Emotional context shapes everything from color choices to information density to copy.
Learning 03
"Being the third designer is a weird privilege."
You inherit the lessons from two previous rounds for free. The failure history is free research. Use it — it's more honest than any client brief.
