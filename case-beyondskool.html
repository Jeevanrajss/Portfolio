Context

A rescue project for kids who need more.

BeyondSkool is a special education platform built for kids with learning needs — personalized learning paths, interactive lessons, progress tracking for parents, and a teaching interface for educators. Built by a Mumbai startup with strong domain expertise.

Like Mr.Med, this project came to me after previous design attempts didn't land with the client.

The Problem

Three users. One product. Wrong approach.

Special education isn't regular education with bigger fonts. The users are different, the learning patterns are different, and the emotional stakes — for kids, parents, and teachers — are much higher. Previous design rounds had treated it like a standard edtech app, and it showed.

"The brief was detailed on features but light on experience. They knew what the platform should do. Figuring out how it should feel was on design."
The Constraints

No margin. High sensitivity. Three audiences.

  • Three distinct user groups in one product: students (kids with learning needs), parents, and teachers — each needed a completely different experience
  • High sensitivity around tone — couldn't feel clinical OR patronizing. A very narrow target
  • Locked feature scope — most functionality was pre-defined by the client, my job was to make it work as an experience
  • Previous design rounds had been rejected — no margin for another miss
My Approach

Study the users, not the features.

Approach 01
Research the actual users, not the persona
Spent time understanding how kids with learning needs actually engage with digital content — attention patterns, visual preferences, friction points. Designed around their reality, not a generic "student" persona.
Approach 02
Separate the three experiences cleanly
Kids got a playful, low-friction interface with strong visual anchors. Parents got calm, data-forward progress views. Teachers got functional, control-heavy dashboards. Three products, one platform.
Approach 03
Warmth without infantilizing
Color palette and illustrations felt friendly but not babyish. Kids with learning needs are still kids — they don't want to be talked down to. Warmth is not the same as patronizing.
The Process

Journey maps first. Screens second.

Mapped the three user journeys separately before touching any visuals. Designed the student experience first — highest stakes, most nuanced — then the parent dashboard, then the teacher interface.

Kept a shared visual language across all three so the product felt unified without feeling identical. The same component system, but the information hierarchy and interaction complexity adapted to each user's context.

Iterated tightly with the PM, incorporated client feedback faster than previous rounds, and built in feedback loops that gave the client visibility at every stage — eliminating the surprise rejections the previous designers had faced.

Impact

Three experiences. One approval.

Learnings

Multi-persona design lessons.

Learning 01
"Specialized user groups need specialized thinking."
You can't design for "kids" as a monolith — especially kids with specific learning needs. Research the actual user before designing for them. Generic personas produce generic products.
Learning 02
"Multi-persona products are really multiple products."
Pretending three different audiences can share one interface is how you end up serving none of them well. The bravery to separate experiences — even within one product — is what makes them work.
Learning 03
"Rescue projects teach you more than clean starts."
Two failed rounds of feedback is two rounds of free insight. The failure history tells you more about what the client actually wants than the original brief ever did. Read before you redesign.
