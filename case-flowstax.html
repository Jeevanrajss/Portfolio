Context

Before FlowStax, banks ran on paper and people.

Loan approvals, account verifications, internal requests — every workflow was manual. Physical forms moved from desk to desk. Validations happened over email. Approvals lived in someone's inbox. One missing signature could stall a process for days.

FlowStax was built to digitize all of that. A no-code workflow platform that takes manual bank operations — verifications, validations, approvals, routing — and turns them into simple, configurable digital workflows.

This was CodeStax's first in-house product. I was the only designer on it.

The Problem

Paper logic into a visual builder. Without breaking anything.

Bank operations teams were drowning in manual work. A single loan application might pass through 8 people across 4 departments, each doing their piece on paper or in siloed tools. Changing a process meant retraining staff, reprinting forms, and praying nothing got lost.

"The brief was big: digitize every manual workflow in the bank, and make it so simple that a business admin — not a developer — can build and change them."

Sounds simple. It's not. You're translating decades of paper-based process logic into a visual builder that non-technical people can actually use without breaking anything.

The Constraints

Two users. One product. Zero precedent.

  • Two very different user groups: the builder (business admin configuring) and the end user (bank employee executing). Both needed to feel native.
  • Massive feature surface area: form builder, flow builder, stage management, promotions, role & permission management, calculations, formulas, notifications — all had to coexist without overwhelming anyone.
  • First product for the company — no design system, no existing patterns, no precedent. Everything from scratch.
  • Bank-grade complexity with consumer-grade usability. Non-negotiable.
  • Replacing muscle memory — staff had done these processes manually for years. The digital version had to feel faster, not just newer.
My Approach

Two products in one. Systems before screens.

Approach 01
Split the mental model early
FlowStax Builder (for admins configuring) and FlowStax App (for employees executing). Same visual language, different interaction depths. Builders get power and density. End users get simplicity and speed.
Approach 02
Map the paper before designing the pixels
I studied the actual manual processes — how a loan form moved through a bank, who verified what, where approvals stalled. The digital version had to respect the logic staff already knew, just remove the friction.
Approach 03
Competitor teardowns
Kissflow, Zapier, Monday, Airtable, Process Street. I mapped how each handled the "simple for business, powerful under the hood" problem. Identified the patterns that worked and the ones that overwhelmed non-technical users.
Approach 04
Systems before screens
Before designing a single flow, I mapped the object model — what's a workflow, what's a stage, what's a trigger, how do they relate. Every UI decision downstream got easier because the underlying logic was clear.
The Process

One grammar. Two experiences.

The end-user app: opinionated and fast. Bank employees don't want to "explore" — they want to finish their tasks and go home. Sidebar navigation, current task front and center, one-click actions, zero hidden features. Replace paper with something faster, not fancier.

The builder: progressive complexity. Simple workflows buildable in minutes with defaults. Complex ones — conditional logic, calculations, role-based permissions — unlock progressively. No upfront complexity. No artificial limits.

Form builder, flow builder, stage builder — all one family. Every builder shares the same drag-drop patterns, the same property panel layout, the same interaction grammar. Learn one, you know them all.

Reusable component library. The first proper component system for CodeStax — cards, tables, form controls, flow nodes, property panels — all reusable across FlowStax and every future product the company would build.

Learnings

What building 0→1 alone teaches you.

Learning 01
"Digitizing manual processes isn't just a UI problem — it's a trust problem."
People who've done something on paper for 20 years don't switch because your product is pretty. They switch because it saves them real time without introducing new ways to mess up. Trust first, delight second.
Learning 02
"Configurable products are a different beast — you're designing a grammar."
Every decision becomes a pattern users hit 1000 times. Small mistakes compound fast. You're not designing screens — you're designing a language that business users will speak daily.
Learning 03
"Power and simplicity aren't opposites — they're layers."
The best no-code tools look easy on the surface and reveal depth only when you need it. Progressive disclosure isn't just a pattern — it's a philosophy of respecting where the user is in their learning journey.
